# Future playable scope and evaluation

This is a plan for later approval. The current deliverable contains no game implementation.

## Gate 1: prove the possession

One modular practice gym, one player, one defender, optional cone, and portrait controls. Include six handle families: basic protective dribble, crossover, between-legs, hesitation, in-and-out, and retreat. Add an attack launch, stop, standing jumper, pull-up, stepback jumper, basic layup, secure two-hand dunk, and one-hand dunk. These are action families, not a promised clip count.

Include the shot clock, fail/okay/perfect release check, baseline stamina, readable defensive commitment, both finishing camera settings, and adjustable haptics. Begin with one original body configuration and a simple practice outfit. Immediate retry matters more than a campaign menu.

The starter outfit is the confirmed white tank and gray joggers with no tattoos. Once basic finishes connect, validate airborne tap-to-layup conversion from the selected dunks. Exclude Euro steps and post offense. Character quality must include the facial/living-motion and fabric review in document 19; testing a simple rig first is not acceptance of a lower final quality floor.

The hard questions are whether a gesture is recognized as intended, whether consecutive actions connect, whether the defender responds believably, and whether the camera preserves control. Do not spend the first experiment on a large shop.

Then validate the owner-requested defensive controls in the same camera: hold/drag positioning, timed swipe/hold block, realistic wrong-way commitment, and recovery. Start with authored attack patterns before a general attacking AI. This is part of the core local experience; live PvP remains later. See document 17.

## Gate 2: prove the full team idea

Add two edge teammates and three assigned defenders. Implement double-tap passing, control transfer after the catch, and a return alley-oop command with one finish skill check. Test realistic interception and failed connection outcomes. Keep off-ball decisions limited to spacing, matchup tracking, and explicit cuts.

Add three contrasting archetypes and seven understandable upgrade categories. Teammates can use the same upgrade screen as the main player. Use pretend currency for economy testing; this gate does not require paid purchases.

## Gate 3: a representative career and wardrobe slice

Represent one high school, one college, and one fictional professional environment through shared kits. Include the home, college, and mansion practice hub progression. Provide a small selection across wardrobe slots and a few original shoe silhouettes with color variants.

Test one complete loop: play → earn → choose an upgrade or cosmetic → practice → return to a challenge. The full forty-school world catalogue and large wardrobe remain expansion content. Career transitions should work before multiplying the schools.

## Gate 4: commercial readiness

Only after the possession is enjoyable: validate store billing, paid/earned balances, item ownership, purchase interruption, refunds, account recovery, saves, support, accessibility, device coverage, privacy disclosures, and regional release requirements. No ads are included. Price testing must not imply that these documents contain approved offers.

Live multiplayer, a full season simulator, unrestricted user-uploaded tattoos, trading, chat, and dozens of bespoke courts are outside the proposed first commercial scope. They can be considered separately without blocking the central game.

## Evaluation plan

All numbers below are initial acceptance hypotheses, not measured results or industry benchmarks.

| Question | Proposed test | Initial target to revisit |
|---|---|---|
| Are gestures understandable? | 10–15 people unfamiliar with the controls, varied hands/phones; log intended and accepted actions | At least 90% can execute crossover, drive, and shot after a short tutorial |
| Are recognizers trustworthy? | Repeated single and chained gestures with intent recorded | Fewer than 5% wrong accepted actions; measure ignored gestures separately |
| Are touch regions safe? | Dribbles near teammate anchors, deliberate passes, accidental double taps | No duplicate command; unintended shot/pass under 1% of eligible trials |
| Is the defender fair? | Show successes and failures without explanation first | Players can explain most losses using visible spacing, timing, or fatigue |
| Do cameras affect difficulty? | Repeat matched scenarios with cinematic and wide settings | Identical simulation/input rules; no systematic timing advantage caused by presentation |
| Does the lob make sense? | Ordinary pass, return lob, blocked lane, exhausted receiver | Players know who they control, who will jump, and why the play failed |
| Is the wardrobe manageable? | Create a look, buy with test currency, equip, return to practice | Complete without tutorial assistance; no hidden stat effects |
| Does performance hold? | Sustained sessions on chosen low/mid/high devices | Target 120 fps on compatible iPhones and a stable 60 fps baseline; establish actual supported profiles from measurement |

For gesture tests, retain sample counts and confusion matrices rather than presenting a single flattering average. Separate touch recognition errors, invalid basketball actions, and valid actions that lost to defense. Repeat across left-handed layouts, aspect ratios, haptics off, and reduced-motion settings.

## Highest-impact risks

The same diagonal could trigger a dribble or lob if start regions are unclear. A cinematic cut could hide a cue or reverse perceived direction. A long canned animation could delay a legal response. Finger placement could hide the ball. A timing check could feel arbitrary if its zones move after the input. Cosmetic layers could clip during deep crouches. Paid stat progression could undermine competitive trust.

Each risk has a specific later test; none has been resolved merely by writing a design rule.
